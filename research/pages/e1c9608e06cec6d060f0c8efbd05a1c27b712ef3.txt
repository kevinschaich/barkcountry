## Timber Sales Available on the Manti-La Sal National Forest

Currently, the Manti-La Sal National Forest has three timber sales available for industry professionals to purchase on the Sanpete Ranger District in Sanpete County, Utah: [Oak](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/forest-products/timber-sales/oak-reoffer-sale), [Knob](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/forest-products/timber-sales/knob-reoffer-timber-sale), and [Lakes](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/forest-products/timber-sales/lakes-timber-sale), respectively. These timber sales are available for purchase ‘off the shelf’ for a limited time through 2026 and 2027 due to lack of competitive bidding when the sales were advertised. Selling timber is a long-held part of the Forest Service mission that provides an essential hands-on approach to reduce fuel loads, improve habitat, combat pests/disease/invasive species, and restore ecological balance and productivity to the forest. Industry professionals are encouraged to review the timber opportunities listed on our [Timber Sales webpage](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/forest-products/timber-sales) – all necessary maps, reports, and information is available. We encourage interested parties to contact the Sanpete Ranger District at any time with inquiries.

## Year-Round Firewood Permits

**Now available online or in person**

Firewood permits on the Manti-La Sal National Forest are now valid from January 1st through December 31st each year and may be purchased online anytime by visiting the [**USDA Forest Service ePermit site**](https://epermits.fs2c.usda.gov/) or in-person at your nearest Ranger District Office. Previously, the permits were not available for online purchase and were only valid from June through November. As a forest, we are pleased to offer this common-sense adjustment which maximizes the usage and convenience of each permit while furthering active forest management, wildfire readiness and response, deregulation and organizational efficiency.

**Firewood Cost & Reminders**

The cost of firewood permits remains unchanged at $5 per cord with a four-cord minimum purchase. The maximum removal limit is 10 cords per individual permit holder per calendar year. Fuelwood permits are required prior to collecting or removing any forest product, including fuelwood, from USDA Forest Service lands. Permit holders must possess a physical copy of the load tag while gathering firewood and note the number of cords collected until the permit is filled. If purchasing online, be sure to print the load tag before heading out to the forest.

Firewood maps for each district are available on the [**Manti-La Sal National Forest’s Forest Products Permits webpage**](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/forest-products/forest-products-industry) or in Avenza Maps. Download Avenza for [**Android**](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details/Avenza_Maps_Offline_Mapping?id=com.Avenza&hl=en) or [**iPhone**](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/avenza-maps-offline-mapping/id388424049), and select the Manti-La Sal firewood maps.

## Annual Conservation Days

Every May, the Manti-La Sal National Forest hosts the Conservation Days educational program for local fourth grade students. Typically, we enjoy the company of 400-500 students from Carbon and Emery Counties as they are led through five interactive stations of hands-on learning  in an outdoor setting. This valuable time together is designed to engage students through environmental education while exploring careers in natural resources. The students, educators and parents who accompany them learn practical knowledge about fish, wildlife, range, fire, heritage, and water resources for a day of outdoor fun that leaves a lasting impression.

Our partners in natural resources protection and education, the Utah Department of Agriculture and Food, Conservation Zone 7, Utah Division of Wildlife, Emery County Recreation Department, and local school districts help make this event an annual success. Without the engagement from the elementary school teachers, administrators and parents of the home study program, this program would not be possible.

## Fuel Moisture Sampling

[![gathering black sagebrush samples](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/nfs/files/styles/full_content/public/r04/manti-lasal/image/q2-newsletter2026-MLSNF-JV-Fuels-Sampling-20260422-93%20%286%29.jpg?itok=5cl5Tqah)](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/nfs/files/r04/manti-lasal/image/q2-newsletter2026-MLSNF-JV-Fuels-Sampling-20260422-93%20%286%29.jpg)

Gathering black sagebrush samples

_(USDA Forest Service, Manti-La Sal National Forest, Holly Shyrer)_

### **Fuels Technician Addy Healy and the Critical Data that Guides Fire Management**

Have you ever wondered how state, government, or local fire programs determine how dry it is across our forests? Within each national forest, dedicated Fuels personnel regularly monitor fuel moisture levels and fuel loading throughout the fire season. These levels are recorded into the [Fire Environment Mapping System](https://fems.fs2c.usda.gov/ui?FieldSample/null/null/3/topo/0/12120/None/None), or FEMS which is a national database referenced by land managers within federal agencies, states, and local governments to make critical decisions around fire, management, resources, and safety during fire season and beyond.

### **What do we mean by ‘fuel’ in Fire and Fuels Management?**

Within the profession of Fire and Fuels Management, “Fuel” refers to all vegetation and organic materials that are available to burn within a landscape, including grasses, shrubs, timber, needles, leaves, logs, and organic soils such as peat or duff. Reducing the amount of fuel through prescribed burning or mechanical fuel reduction such as thinning improves the resilience of forests and rangelands to wildfire, insect outbreaks, plant invasions, and other disturbances. Since fuel across a landscape provides the energy that sustains a fire and contributes to how quickly and intensely it spreads, the moisture level within that fuel is critical to monitor in a consistent and repeatable way.  Being able to review and measure fuel moisture levels is essential for land managers to review and for timely decision making as new data comes in and conditions change. Throughout the summer, managers across Utah and the nation rely on the fuels data collected by field technicians to guide staffing, response, and preparedness efforts that protect our communities and natural resources.

### **A day in the field with a Fuels Technician**

Fuels Management is a science, and like any field, it has its complexities which require trained and educated employees to understand and observe various protocols, standards, and regulations used to capture the data on the ground. Recently, Fuels Technician Addy Healy walked us through how the Forest Service measures fuel moisture levels at the Joe’s Valley sampling site on the Manti–La Sal National Forest. These measurements update FEMS, the national database that tracks fuel moisture levels during fire season. The forest has four fuel moisture monitoring sites that are surveyed every two weeks and contribute to the fuel moisture data across Utah. This data helps land managers know exactly how dry the landscape is so they can make accurate decisions around fire, resources, and safety.

During our field time, Addy showed us how to collect samples of live plants, dead vegetation, and duff required to complete the fuel moisture sampling protocol. At each of the three stations in the Joe’s Valley area, we gathered our designated fuel types, which included:

- 100‑hour dead fuels (dead branches size 1-3 inches in diameter)
- 1,000‑hour dead fuels (branches and logs size 3–8‑inches in diameter)
- Duff, which is the layer of decomposed organic material on the forest floor
- Live samples from needles of subalpine fir, ponderosa pine, Engelmann spruce, scale leaves from Utah Juniper, and leaves from black sagebrush.

Each sample was collected in the field, placed in a designated container, and weighed upon returning to the office. The samples were then dried in an oven at 150–250°F for 24 hours. After drying, each sample was weighed again, and the difference in weight was used to determine its moisture content. These results are entered into the national FEMS database, where land managers use the data to assess current fuel moisture conditions and evaluate how readily wildfire could spread.

It’s important to note that the numbers associated with fuel moisture levels don’t label fuel moisture as “good” or “bad.” Instead, they serve as neutral, informative data points that help land managers understand trends, assess risk, and make well informed decisions around fire and fuels protocols. As the season progresses, Fuels Technicians across the nation will continue to record the fuels moisture levels until snow closes out the fire season. Once recorded, the year’s data will stay available within the FEMS database, and will combine with previous and subsequent years to reveal moisture level trends over time. The ability to capture and record this data is one of the first and most essential steps in our world of Fire and Fuels; big thanks to Addy for giving us the opportunity to peek over her shoulder as she worked and for her willingness to explain the process, protocol, and importance that fuels moisture levels play during the hot summer season ahead.

## Safety and Livestock

Most of us know that livestock grazing on the Wasatch Plateau has been part of the landscape for well over a century. An important aspect for many livestock growers is their access to Manti-La Sal National Forest lands for permitted grazing opportunities. By working closely with grazing permittees, the Rangeland Management program monitors on-the-ground conditions to promote plant diversity and overall rangeland health, encouraging a healthy mix of shrubs, forbs, and grasses for both livestock and wildlife. Well managed grazing paired with prescribed fire and thinning can reduce fine fuels such as grasses that may otherwise contribute to wildfire spread; these efforts improve wildfire readiness across large landscapes for our communities. As summer heats up and recreationists make their way to the forest, it’s important to remember that cattle, sheep, sheep dogs, herders and cattlemen are a common occurrence on forest roads. Below are some fast facts and safety tips to make recreating around livestock easier.

### Herders, Roads, and Camping Tips

**Livestock Herders**

Sheepherders and cowboys often live with their herds 24/7, their camps are authorized to stay longer than the 14-day limit. Herds will often have livestock guardian dogs, these large white or tan dogs are working; do not approach, feed, or attempt to ‘rescue’ the dogs. Depending on conditions and location, you can expect to see livestock on the forest from June through October.

**Livestock on Roads**

Forest roads are high-activity zones. Slow down on blind corners and yield to herds. Do not honk, as it can spook animals and injure riders. Keep in mind that some areas are open range, which means you could encounter livestock on the road at any time; be particularly careful during times of low visibility such as dawn, dusk, at night, and during bad weather.

**Livestock and Camping**

You may encounter livestock in dispersed camping areas, but most designated campgrounds are fenced to keep livestock out. Don’t camp near troughs or corrals, and keep in mind that fencing, corrals, troughs, and pipelines are essential and must not be tampered with. Never block access to gates or water sources with vehicles.

## New Programmatic Agreement with Utah State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO)

Utah’s National Forests are home to historic recreation residence tracts and summer homes that tell the story of generations who have enjoyed these landscapes. Maintaining these properties is important—not only for the families who use them but also for preserving their cultural and historic value for future generations.

To make this process easier and more efficient, the Manti-La Sal National Forest is partnering with the Utah State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO) and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation to develop a Programmatic Agreement (PA) in 2026. This agreement will streamline the review process for maintenance and improvement projects on historic properties across Utah’s National Forests while ensuring their historic character remains intact. More information on this valuable partnership is available on our [Partnerships page](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/manti-lasal/working-with-us/partnerships/programmatic-agreement-utah-state-historic).

## The USDA Forest Service Reorganization

### On March 31st the Forest Service announced its reorganization plan.

As part of the USDA reorganization plan, the Forest Service is realigning its organizational structure to strengthen local leadership, streamline operations, and improve mission delivery. The changes include moving headquarters to Salt Lake City, transitioning to a state-based leadership model, building a network of Operations Service Centers, and unifying the agency’s research program.

The Manti-La Sal National Forest is committed to ensuring that all operations — including wildfire readiness and response — continue without interruption. The Forest Service’s fire readiness and response remain unchanged, and our operational firefighters and aviation resources continue to support wildfire response.
