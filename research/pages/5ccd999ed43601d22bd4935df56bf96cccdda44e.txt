(Photo courtesy Briana Moore. Used with permission.) <br>## Enjoying Acadia With Your Pet<br>Exploring Acadia with your pet can be an exciting and rewarding experience. Natural areas pose unique challenges and risks. Please take a moment to review these guidelines to keep your pet, other visitors, and wildlife safe.<br>- Become an [Acadia Bark Ranger](https://www.nps.gov/articles/be-an-acadia-bark-ranger.htm).<br>- Federal law requires that all pets must be kept on a leash no longer than 6 feet (2 m) at all times. Wildlife, including porcupines and those with infectious diseases, can harm even well-trained pets. The presence of unleashed pets running loose also can be highly disturbing to unseen wildlife. Please protect your pet, other visitors, and the park by keeping your pet on a leash.<br>- Collect and dispose properly of animal waste in designated trash receptacles. Pet waste can leech into groundwater and pass diseases to wildlife.<br>- Do not leave your pet unattended, including at campsites or in cars. Summer sunshine poses a threat to pets in vehicles.<br>- Remember to check yourself and your pet for [ticks](https://www.nps.gov/acad/planyourvisit/safety.htm).<br>- Planning a hike? Be sure to avoid ladder and rung trails, and bring plenty of water.<br>### Service Animals<br>[Service animals](https://www.nps.gov/acad/planyourvisit/service-animals.htm) may accompany their owners to all park locations and programs.<br>National Park Service policy defines a service animal as a dog that has been individually trained to do work or perform tasks for the benefit of an individual with a disability, including a physical, sensory, psychiatric, intellectual, or other mental disability.<br>- The tasks performed by the animal must be directly related to the person’s disability. Emotional support, therapy, comfort or companion animals are not considered service animals.<br>  <br>- Learn much more about [service animals in national parks.](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/accessibility/service-animals.htm)<br>### Where to Go (and Where Not To)<br>There are 100 miles (161 km) of hiking trails and 45 miles (72 km) of carriage roads in the park where pets are permitted. Pets and people may not swim in them.<br>  <br>- Sand Beach (June 15-Sept 8) and Echo Lake (May 15-Sept 15) are off-limits to pets.<br>  <br>- Public buildings<br>  <br>- Ranger-led programs<br>  <br>- Wild Gardens of Acadia (Sieur de Monts)<br>  <br>- Duck Harbor Campground<br>  <br>#### Park Trails Closed to Pets<br>- Precipice<br>  <br>- Beehive<br>  <br>- Ladder Trail to Dorr Mountain<br>  <br>- Beech Cliffs Trail<br>  <br>- Perpendicular Trail (Mansell Mountain)<br>  <br>- Jordan Cliffs Trail between Penobscot East Trail and the carriage road<br>  <br>#### Park Trails Not Recommended for Pets<br>- Acadia Mountain<br>  <br>- Flying Mountain<br>  <br>- Giant Slide<br>  <br>- Cadillac Mountain- west face<br>  <br>- Bubble and Jordan Ponds Path, between the carriage road and The Featherbed pond<br>  <br>- Norembega Goat Trail<br>  <br>- Bubbles-Pemetic Trail<br>  <br>- Penobscot Mountain (Spring) Trail<br>  <br>- Upper Beachcroft Trail<br>  <br>- Upper Gorge Trail<br>  <br>![Two photos of dog poop bags left by a sign and garbage can](https://www.nps.gov/acad/planyourvisit/images/Dog-Bag-and-Take-examples.jpg?maxwidth=650&autorotate=false)Keep the park clean by collecting and disposing of all dog waste. Do not leave it behind at a trailhead or next to garbage cans. <br>## Dispose of Pet Waste Properly<br>Help keep Acadia clean by collecting and disposing of all pet waste properly.
