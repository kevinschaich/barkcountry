**Chapter I - Introduction**

**PURPOSE AND NEED**

The Northern Great Plains Management Plans Revision is a combined planning effort by
three national forests: the Custer, Medicine Bow/Routt, and Nebraska. These national
forests administer the following national grassland and national forest units, all of
which are affected by this revision process. They are:

**Custer National Forest Units**

Cedar River National Grassland

Grand River National Grassland

Little Missouri National Grassland

Sheyenne National Grassland

**Medicine Bow-Routt National Forest Unit**

Thunder Basin National Grassland

**Nebraska National Forest Units**

Bessey Ranger District/Samuel R. McKelvie National Forest

Buffalo Gap National Grassland

Fort Pierre National Grassland

Oglala National Grassland

Pine Ridge Ranger District

Land and resource management plans (management plans) currently direct management of
the national forests and their units. Issuance of these plans occurred: June 10, 1987, for
the Custer National Forest; November 20, 1985, for the Medicine Bow National Forest, and
December 14, 1984, for the Nebraska National Forest. Other National Forest System units
under the administration of the Custer and Medicine Bow-Routt National Forests that are
not listed above will be addressed in future planning efforts.

Revision of management plans is directed by the National Forest Management Act (NFMA),
regulations 35 CFR 219 and the Forest Service Directives System (FSH 1909.12). Three
proposed revised management plans (one for each participating national forest) and one
Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS) describing environmental effects for all three
participating national forests will be completed and available for public review.

The purpose of the Analysis of the Management Situation (AMS) document is to describe
past and present land uses and current management direction. The AMS contains the results
and conclusions from various assessment studies, inventories and monitoring reports, which
describe the current condition and uses of resources. According to 36 CFR 219.12(e), the
AMS should describe:

� The current level of goods and services provided and the most likely amount to be
provided if current management continues;

� Projections of demand;

� A determination of the potential to resolve public issues and management;

� A determination of the need to establish or change management direction; and

� Benchmark analyses to define the range within which alternatives can be constructed.

In essence, these assessment studies and monitoring reports help Forest Service
managers develop a balance of goods, services, opportunities and habitat conditions and
types over time, which assure sustainable economies and environments, and allow all
segments of the public a

chance to use and enjoy national grasslands and national forests and to the fullest
extent possible.

Because ecosystems cross boundaries (whether they be federal, state, tribal, county or
private), the agency will look beyond boundaries to gain a "big picture" sense
of how the Northern Great Plains ecosystem is functioning.

Assessments in and of themselves do not lead to decisions made in a management plan.
The assessments provide a synthesis of information that will lead to development of
alternative ways to manage these lands. The "broadscale" assessments are rich
sources of information to glimpse the "big picture" of the Northern Great Plains
ecosystem. Well-grounded information is critical to making sound land and resource
management decisions. Some of the more important assessments include: an aquatic/water
quality study, a broadscale vegetation study, a grassland vegetative structure study, a
roadless inventory, a recreation analysis, a regional prairie dog study, a threatened,
endangered and sensitive species broadscale assessment, a wild and scenic river
evaluation, and a wildlife habitat suitability study.

Through these assessments and other analyses, grassland and forest managers will be
better able to balance the many needs and desires expressed by a diverse public. When the
time comes to make a final decision on the directions to be taken on these public lands in
the future, the assessments will also provide valuable information upon which to base
decisions.

**A Summary of Ecosystem Characteristics:**

**A Broadscale Look at the Northern Great Plains**

To better understand and appreciate the significance of the Northern Great Plains, its
people, and the ecosystems at work, some general descriptions and bulleted statements at a
broadscale level follow. For this discussion, the Northern Great Plains is the grassland
and shrubland dominated areas of Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Wyoming, and
northern and central Nebraska.

**General Broadscale Findings**

The following broadscale findings were taken from interpretations of the Northern Great
Plains Steppe Assessment Map. The Nature Conservancy commissioned the Augustana College
and Hughes STX Corporation, both in Sioux Falls, SD, to delineate all "intact"
natural grassland areas larger than 50 square miles in the U.S. Northern Great Plains and
to make a visual assessment of the composition, integrity and quality. Three levels of
quality (high, moderate and low) were assigned based on observed historic or current land
use management.

� Few large grassland landscapes--areas greater than 50 square miles of
non-intensively managed grasslands--exist in the eastern half of the Northern Great
Plains. The Sheyenne National Grassland is one of the few remaining and is considered to
be of moderate quality. Generally, native grasslands in the eastern half of the Northern
Great Plains are small "postage stamp" prairies.

� In North Dakota, the Little Missouri National Grassland and the Theodore Roosevelt
National Park represent the largest intact natural grassland areas in the state. Large
grassland patches north of this area, including northern Great Plains area in Canada, are
not present due to small-grain farming.

� In South Dakota, the Missouri River is the dividing line between two major land-use
practices--cropping in the east and livestock grazing in the west. About one-third of all
high-quality grassland landscapes in the Northern Great Plains exist in western South
Dakota.

� Nebraska's high-quality grassland landscapes exist in the Sandhills region. East of
the Sandhills, cropping practices are common, while livestock grazing predominates to the
west.

� Wyoming has several large grassland landscapes in the Northern Great Plains,
including the largest at approximately 11,600 square miles of good quality with 90 percent
of it in grass and shrubland. The Thunder Basin National Grassland lies within this
landscape.

**A Broadscale Look at People on the Northern Great Plains**

**_12,000 Years to 1700 A.D_.**

� Paleo-Indians live on the Northern Great Plains, gathering plants and hunting game.

� Numerous native cultures thrive, and gradually evolve new cultural systems. Many
reside in villages near rivers. Some cultivate the land, growing domesticated crops.
Others live off the land in nomadic bands and develop bison-hunting skills. Many use fire
as a tool to alter the vegetative make-up of the land or to drive game. Long-term droughts
affect the sustainability of the different cultures, with hunting and gathering cultures
surviving climatic changes better than cultures dependent on domesticated crops. The first
contacts between American Indians and Europeans/Euro-Americans are made.

**_1700 to 1860 A.D._**

� Many American Indian tribes acquire the knowledge and use of the horse. Using the
horse, these people adapt their hunting practices to effectively hunt large grazers,
especially bison, upon which their cultures depend. The first wave of Europeans enter the
Northern Great Plains, most of them trappers and traders involved in the fur trade. Trade
systems between the tribes and fur traders rise, then later decline. Isolated fur trading
posts are built. Diseases, such as small pox, are brought by some traders to tribal
villages. Some tribes, like the Mandan, are devastated by such diseases.

**_1860 to 1890 A.D._**

� The first of many homestead acts is passed by Congress. Cattlemen, farmers and
miners begin to settle the region. Lands especially east of the Missouri River undergo
significant cultivation. The open-range cattle industry rises and falls. Railroad building
begins in earnest. Railroad construction results in hundreds of thousands of acres begin
given to land-grant railroads by the federal government. Much of this acreage is sold to
private individuals. Conflicts erupt between new settlers and American Indian tribes.
Conflicts also arise between cattlemen and sheepmen, and cattlemen and homesteaders as
they all compete for available range and farmland. Within a couple decades, tribal people
are forced onto reservations and the once great bison herds are reduced to several hundred
animals.

**_1890 to 1940 A.D._**

� Resource use increases rapidly. Homesteading and the fencing of the range increases.
Foreign immigration swells; many small towns are formed on the Northern Great Plains.
Federal land policies encourage crop production. The droughts of the 1890s and 1930s
create "dust bowl" conditions. Federal conservation laws are enacted to correct
improper land uses and to restore the health of the land. Between 1933 and 1940, 11
million acres of land are purchased by the federal government as a way to restore land and
to assist desperate farmers and ranchers suffering from economic hardships. Aquifers are
tapped for irrigation and rivers for municipal purposes. Oil, coal and natural gas
production becomes increasingly important on the Northern Great Plains. Some species, like
the gray wolf, grizzly bear, and Audubon bighorn sheep, become extinct.

**_1940 to 1960 A.D._**

� Many people leave the Northern Great Plains to seek economic opportunities
elsewhere. Grassland and wetland conservation continues. Twelve percent of the nation's
grasslands are reorganized under federal authority. Livestock grazing associations become
established. Congress authorizes the Pick-Sloan Missouri Basin Program in 1944, which
leads to the construction of major reservoirs, such as Garrison and Oahe, on the Missouri
River.

**_1960 A.D. to the present_**

� Significant conservation and environmental legislation is passed by Congress.
Multiple-use sustained-yield concepts are adopted on national forests and grasslands.
International demands for grain, especially wheat, increase, providing opportunities for
farmers to utilize advances in production agriculture to increase cultivation. The
Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) is adopted to take marginal farmlands out of
production. Most areas on the Northern Great Plains continue to lose population. Tourism
and recreation grow in popularity on public lands. Native Americans are increasing and
regaining their cultural identity.

**_Where We Are Now:_**

� Farming has changed the vegetative pattern across the landscape. Farmers within the
Northern Great Plains contribute significantly to the national and world food supply.

� Crop and livestock production is a major economic base for most rural communities.

� Irrigation, dam building, stream rechanneling and the tapping of aquifers have
effects on crop production, flood control, and increased opportunities for lake
recreation. Such alterations also have had effects on fish and wildlife resources,
wetlands and aquifers, stream flow regimes and conversions of grasslands to croplands.

� Land use alterations have reduced Great Plains grasslands to about one to four
percent of the tallgrass prairie, 34 percent of the mixed-grass prairie, and 26 percent of
the short-grass prairie.

� Land use alterations and exotic plant and animal introductions have had adverse
effects on many native species; other native species have adapted and increased in
numbers.

� Public is becoming increasingly concerned about the importance of native prairies.

� Opportunities to view prairie landscapes without evidence of human manipulation or
development are limited.

� People voice concerns about both the sustainability of their lifestyles and the
quality of their environment.

� Coal development, railroad development, and oil and gas development have had a
positive economic impact on the Northern Great Plains.

� Populations on the Northern Great Plains have remained relatively stable since about
1930\. In 1930, the total population for North and South Dakota, Nebraska and Wyoming was
approximately 2,978,000. In 1990, the total population was approximately 3,367,000.

� Hunting, fishing, hiking and other forms of recreation are generally increasing on
the Northern Great Plains. Tourism is helping diversify local economies near several
national grasslands and forests.

� Collaborative planning, involving citizens, governments and agencies, is viewed by
federal agencies as the best future strategy for managing public lands on the Northern
Great Plains with the greatest potential for public support and acceptance.

**A Broadscale Look at Climatic and Geologic Relationships on the**

**Northern Great Plains**

**_The Climate in General_**

In general, the climate on the Northern Great Plains is influenced by three major air
masses: the Pacific, the Arctic, and the Gulf air masses. "The relative dominance of
these air masses cycles with the seasons and movement of the jet stream from north to
south" (von Ahlefeldt 2-22). Furthermore, it's fair to say that a good deal of
climatic variation exists.

The climate of the Northern Great Plains can be described as continental, with low
relative humidity, and abundant sunshine. Precipitation is generally moderate to low, with
much of it originating in the Gulf of Mexico or from the west. Drier units lie in the
western sections of the study region. Arctic winds arriving from the north and northwest
make winters quite cold and hazardous. Drought conditions periodically occur across the
region. Weather conditions during any season can change very rapidly.

**_Furthermore_**

� Past geological and climatic events, such as glaciation, created a variety of soils,
many of them deep, in a varied but mostly rolling landscape.

� More precipitation falls during the summer than in the winter, mainly as a result of
moisture patterns arising in the Gulf of Mexico.

� More precipitation falls in eastern than western portions of the Northern Great
Plains because of the "rain shadow" effect of the Rocky Mountains.

� The Northern Great Plains is under drought conditions at least 25 percent of the
time. Once droughts occur, they commonly last for two or more years.

� Annual fluctuations in growing season temperature and precipitation can be extreme.

� Wind blows constantly throughout the Northern Great Plains.

� Available shallow aquifers occur beneath portions of the Northern Great Plains; in
other areas, available groundwater may be extremely limited.

**_As a Result_**

� Glaciation and wind and water erosion have created a variety of soil types and
varied topography.

� The diversity of native plant life is a reflection of the diversity of soil types
and variations in climate.

� The Northern Great Plains is generally well suited to grass production and grazing.

� More precipitation in eastern portions of the Great Plains gives rise to tallgrass
prairies; mid- and short-grass prairies lie primarily west of the 100th Meridian.

� Most native plants have adapted to tolerate temperature and moisture extremes by
growing extensive root systems.

� Low annual rainfall holds nutrients in the top portion of the soil, creating a rich
soil matrix beneficial to plant production.

**_This Means_**

� Much of the Northern Great Plains has been developed for agricultural purposes.

� Management of rangelands for livestock forage, wildlife habitats and watershed
values is complicated by the extreme variability in annual weather patterns.

� Drought has affected the lifestyles and livelihoods of people for centuries--from
Paleo-Indians to today's ranchers and farmers.

� Generally, exposed soils are highly erodible on the Northern Great Plains. Some of
these soils are susceptible to compaction.

� Grass production may vary as much as 50 percent based on changes in annual weather
conditions.

**A Broadscale Look at Rangeland and Forest Health on the**

**Northern Great Plains**

**_150 Years Ago_**

� Tallgrass, mixed grass, and short-grass prairies dominate the Northern Great Plains
vegetation.

� Grasslands are extensive and continuous.

� Millions of bison, elk, deer and pronghorn antelope interact with weather, fire,
prairie dogs and other wildlife to shape a diverse mosaic of native vegetation across the
landscape.

� Frequent fires every five to thirty years restrict the expansion of woody plants,
release nutrients into the soil, affect species diversity, animal communities and travel
routes. Fires are both human and natural caused.

� Grizzly bears, gray wolves and mountain lions serve as the grasslands' top
predators.

� Fire maintains ponderosa pine and Rocky Mountain juniper areas as savannas rather
than thick forests. Hardwood tree species and associate wildlife species are not abundant.

� Fire, grazing animals, drought, floods, hail and other disturbances affect plant
species composition and productivity.

� Prairie dog colonies cover extensive areas.

**_Over the Last 150 Years_**

� Much of the native grassland is cultivated; most of the remainder provides
pasturelands for grazing animals.

� Native sagebrush plains are reduced by herbicide, chained or burned.

� Native grasslands and shrublands are dissected by fences, roads, railroads and
utility corridors.

� The amount of fencing has increased, often to control the movement of livestock or
to improve rangeland condition.

� Major conservation programs to repair eroded lands are implemented during and after
the Dust Bowl era, circa 1940 to 1960.

� Agricultural conservation practices are developed which control or reduce wind and
soil erosion.

� Prairie tree and shrub planting programs, including planting shelter belts and wind
breaks and tree planting in towns and cities, are implemented.

� Domestic livestock replace bison and other large native herbivores as the dominant
grazers.

� Wolves and many other large predators are eliminated with increase human activity.

� Cultivation and other land uses have reduced the habitats of native species, such as
prairie dogs.

� Black-footed ferret populations are substantially eliminated through habitat loss
and disease.

� The natural disturbance regimes of fire, flooding and grazing are substantially
altered in a relatively short period of time.

� Euro-American settlement introduces new diseases that threaten native plant and
animal species; plague is introduced from Asia and prairie dogs prove to be very
susceptible to the new disease.

� Crop and livestock production become major land uses.

� Hunting, fishing, hiking and other forms of recreation increase on rangelands.

� Rangeland conservation develops as a science, introducing new ways to manage
livestock grazing and address rangeland health.

**_As a Result_**

� Grazing by large native herbivores, such as bison, elk, deer, and pronghorn, has
been greatly reduced, but is replaced by domestic livestock. The dietary requirements and
utilization of vegetation by cattle differs from those of native herbivores.

� Food and grain crops replaces native prairies and plowing exposes soil to wind and
run-off. Exotic and non-native plants replace native vegetation in many places.

� Conservation efforts help restore some areas damaged during the Dust Bowl.

� The native vegetative mosaic across the landscape has been substantially altered and
plant and animal relationships that once evolved over time are changed in a relatively
short period of time.

� Improved transportation increases human use and impacts on the land and its
resources.

� Prairie dog populations and the wildlife community they support--including
black-footed ferrets--are at risk in many areas, especially as a result of population
reductions and the increasing threat of plague.

� Declines in the 330 species of breeding birds on the Great Plains have occurred,
with some species registering 91 percent reductions. Other species have flourished.

� Many areas of ponderosa pine, Rocky Mountain juniper, and oak savanna change to
heavily forested areas, primarily as a result of fire suppression.

� Crop and livestock production is a major economic base for most rural communities.

**_This Means_**

� The diversity of the ecosystem has changed over time and space due to control of
natural disturbance processes, such as fires and floods, and the differences in grazing
patterns and behavior between nomadic, native grazing animals and domesticated livestock.

� Native grassland and shrubland ecosystems are fragmented.

� The mix of wildlife species has changed and many native and endemic species have
decreased.

� The threat of catastrophic fire has increased as savannas convert to thick forests.

� New stands of timber are available for commercial use in some locations.

� Local economies are bolstered through timber harvest, crop and livestock production,
and mineral development. Improved livestock management practices and agricultural
practices help conserve soil and water resources and contribute to the sustainability of
those practices.

� Tourism and recreation income helps diversify local economies.

� Restoration of viable populations of threatened and endangered plant and animal
species and control of noxious weeds become of great public interest.

� Most of the native uplands of the National Grasslands on the northern Great Plains
contain all the parts of vegetative composition. Rangeland professionals generally agree
that these grasslands are in better condition today than at the turn of the century - when
unregulated grazing and drought caused extensive rangeland degradation. Improvement may be
needed. Whereas rangeland actions in the past decades have focused on forage production,
forage availability for livestock is not necessarily consistent with the desired
conditions for rangelands.

**A Broadscale Look at Aquatic Ecosystems on the Northern Great Plains**

**_150 Years Ago_**

� River and natural wetland basins play a vital role in the hydrologic cycle.

� Most streams and rivers on the Northern Great Plains are free-flowing, highly
meandering, and slowly moving.

� Most streams and rivers are exposed to sunlight, causing high summer temperatures
and high salinity from evaporation. Hardwood trees in riparian areas are not abundant.

� The water flow within most rivers seasonally fluctuates due to weather conditions.
Floods resulting from high runoff in the late spring and early summer frequently occur,
scouring streamside zones.

� Soils are able to store and disperse water underground.

� Many perennial streams run clear during late-season runoffs because streamside
vegetation catches instream sediments. Other rivers, like the Little Missouri, carry heavy
sediment loads all year long and never run clear.

� Erosion occurs based on disturbance processes, such as fire and grazing. Along many
streams, trailing by bison causes eroded stream banks.

� Many large rivers are wide, shallow and turbid due to tributary runoff.

� Aquifers, including the High Plains, Northern Great Plains, and Great Plains
Systems, extend from Nebraska to Canada, and from Iowa to the Missouri River.

� Aquatic animals consist of native fish species and other native reptiles and
amphibians.

� Streamside vegetation is at times burned by wild fires or heavily grazed by bison
and other wild ungulates, resulting in little woody vegetation alongside some streams.

**_Over the Last 150 Years_**

� A great deal of water is annually withdrawn and diverted from streams and rivers.
Many rivers are rechanneled. Many wetlands are drained, filled or cultivated.

� Agricultural, municipal and industrial waste enters many streams, rivers and
groundwater supplies.

� A whole array of non-native species, including sportfish and baitfish, are
introduced into the aquatic environment, providing public fishing opportunities.

� Dams, levees and impoundments are built to reduce or moderate stream flows and
control severe flooding within some major river systems.

� Stockpond and reservoirs substantially increase.

� Multiple small impoundments are constructed in most watersheds.

� Most grazing along streams has changed from periodic and nomadic grazing by wild
ungulates to confined or continuous grazing by domesticated livestock.

**_As a Result_**

� The diversity and range of native aquatic plant and animal species have decreased.
Some native fish species are cut-off from their historic migration routes. Non-native
plant and animal aquatic species have increased.

� Turbidity and suspended solids have increased in some areas from farmland plowing,
while turbidity below dams has decreased.

� Intermittent flooding is controlled and water is diverted. Water is removed from
aquifers for cropland irrigation.

� Instream flows are decreasing in many streams and rivers.

� Water quality is decreasing.

� Dams, stockponds and wells provide benefits to people, including drinking water,
flood control, irrigation for crops, water for a variety of wildlife species, and
increased utilization of rangelands for livestock grazing.

� The natural flow of water through most watersheds is altered.

� New aquatic habitat is created.

**_This Means_**

� Water quality and safety are issues in some areas and water supplies for many
communities are in question.

� Some water pollutants pose health risks.

� Recreational opportunities associated with reservoirs become important economic and
lifestyle considerations to local communities.

� Risks associated with flooding decrease in many areas and increase in some areas.

� Native aquatic plant and animal habitat is declining at an accelerated rate due to
deterioration, and competition and predation from non-native aquatic species.

� Fish species more suited to less turbid water, such as below dams, out-compete some
native species dependent upon turbid water.

� Controls on water flow are reducing wetlands. Some water wells have dried up because
of subsurface water removal.

� New recreational fisheries are created, including fisheries in stock ponds on the
national grasslands.

� Losses of presettlement wetlands register about 40 to 60 percent in North Dakota,
and between 20 to 40 percent in South Dakota, Nebraska and Wyoming. More than 90 percent
of all presettlement wetlands in tallgrass prairies has been lost.

**A Summary of Ecosystem Characteristics:**

**A Landscape Look at the Northern Great Plains**

To further explore the significance of the Northern Great Plains, its people, and the
ecosystems at work, some additional discussions and bulleted statements at a more
localized landscape level are provided.

**Custer National Forest Units:**

**Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands**

Located in Grant and Sioux Counties of North Dakota, the Cedar River National Grassland
is a 6,700-acre tract of mixed-grass prairie on rolling hills, intersected by streams and
dry gullies. Most of this unit lies within the interior boundaries of the Standing Rock
Indian Reservation. The Grassland is administered by the Grand River/Cedar River Ranger
District, Lemmon, South Dakota. The Cedar River National Grassland is managed for multiple
purposes, including livestock grazing. The last significant buffalo hunt occurred near the
grassland in 1883, when a group of Sioux and whites harvested about 10,000 head.

Located in Perkins and Corson Counties of South Dakota, the Grand River National
Grassland contains more than 155,000 acres and is administered by the Grand River/Cedar
River Ranger District, Lemmon, South Dakota. Mixed-grass vegetation rises from its rolling
landscape. The unit is home to pronghorn and mule and white-tailed deer. Nearby Shadehill
Reservoir provides good fishing, camping and boating recreation. Long ago, one of the
area's first white trappers, Hugh Glass, entered the annals of history near the grassland.
In 1823, Glass fended off a mother grizzly, only to be terribly mauled. He then crawled
200 miles over six weeks, finally reaching Fort Kiowa on the Missouri River, where he
recovered.

_Landscape Conditions on the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands_

� In South Dakota, the Missouri River is the dividing line between two major land-use
practices--cropping in the east and livestock grazing in the west. About one-third of all
high-quality grassland landscapes in the Northern Great Plains exist in western South
Dakota, which includes the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands.

� Varied and abundant fossil resources exist on the grasslands.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands_

� Overall, native grasslands remain in a high-quality condition. This includes a
little over 10 percent of the area in crested wheatgrass and less than one percent in
leafy spurge.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands_

� Many water impoundments have been constructed, which provides more utilization of
forage by livestock but at the same time changes water flows and grassland conditions.

_Recreation on the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands_

� Big game and upland bird hunting is popular, as well as prairie dog shooting.

� Birdwatching is an emerging activity.

_Economic Environment on the Cedar and Grand River National Grasslands_

� Livestock grazing is important to the lifestyle of the local communities.

**Little Missouri National Grassland**

The Little Missouri National Grassland, at more than a million acres, is the largest
national grassland in the nation. This mixed-grass prairie found in badlands topography is
located in McKenzie, Billings, Slope, and Golden Valley Counties in western North Dakota.
The Grassland is administered by the McKenzie Ranger District, Watford City, North Dakota,
and the Medora Ranger District, Dickinson, North Dakota.

The Little Missouri is home to a great variety of wildlife, including bighorn sheep,
eagles and falcons, prairie dogs, and pronghorn antelope. Oil and gas production and
livestock grazing are important on this unit, as are opportunities for remote roadless
experiences.

_Landscape Conditions on the Little_

_Missouri National Grassland_

� The Little Missouri National Grassland and Theodore Roosevelt National Park
represent the largest natural intact grasslands in the state.

� Fire suppression has increased cool season and exotic plant species; shrubs and
trees have increased.

� Most of the native uplands on the Little Missouri National Grassland contain all the
parts of vegetative composition. Rangeland professionals generally agree that these
grasslands are in better condition today than at the turn of the century - when
unregulated grazing and drought caused extensive rangeland degradation.

� At least 15,500 acres of leafy spurge and nearly 35,000 acres of crested wheat grass
occur on this grassland.

� Oil and gas is found on the grassland, with exploration and production most recently
booming in the 1980s.

� Sizable fossil resources exist on the grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Little Missouri National Grassland_

� An extensive badlands system provides a scenic backdrop for the grassland.

� Almost all national grassland areas are grazed in the summer by livestock each year,
resulting in fairly uniform cover levels after the grazing season.

� Recent monitoring indicates low levels of high structure (tall grass at the end of
the grazing season). Less than five percent is in high structure on the rolling prairie
landtype.

� Many wooded draws and riparian woodlands are regenerating under improved management,
but about a third continue to decline or fail to regenerate. In addition to loss of
watershed values and recreational values, this also reduces habitat for deer and many
species of birds.

� Sagebrush habitat on the national grassland is limited but is important for
wintering pronghorns and other wildlife species.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on and near the Little Missouri National Grassland_

� Constraining and impounding the Missouri River have reduced the sediment load, in
turn reducing the numbers of species that require more turbid water and increasing those
species that require clearer water.

� Spawning areas have been reduced due to impoundments, flow changes and sediment
loading.

_Recreation on the Little Missouri National Grassland_

� The construction of the Maah Daah Hey Trail will increase opportunities for
horseback riders, hikers, and mountain bike riders.

� Mountain biking is an emerging activity.

� A number of roadless areas provide for excellent backcountry recreation.

� Big game and upland bird hunting are popular, as are photography, hiking, wildlife
viewing, and shooting prairie dogs.

� During high flows, canoeing is popular along the Little Missouri River, while
snowmobiling has been popular during the cold months. The Little Missouri is a state
scenic river.

_Economic Environment on the Little Missouri National Grassland_

� The Little Missouri National Grassland contains the largest grazing program and oil
and gas leasing program of any of the administrative units in the National Forest System.

� Several counties depend on livestock grazing for employment. Of these, Billings
County, at six percent, is the highest employment dependency associated with national
grassland activities in the Northern Great Plains.

� Billings and McKenzie Counties are dependent on income from oil and gas leasing.
About 22 percent of the income in Billings County is due to oil and gas leasing activities
on the national grassland (the highest income of any of the affected counties in the
planning area).

� Payments to counties have been the largest (more than $2 million per year the past
decade) of any national grassland in the Northern Great Plains, mainly due to oil and gas
leasing.

**Sheyenne National Grassland**

The more than 70,000 acres of the Sheyenne National Grassland consists of tallgrass
prairie, oak savanna and river woodlands in southeastern North Dakota, including parts of
Ransom and Richland Counties. This unit is administered by the Sheyenne Ranger District in
Lisbon.

The Sheyenne National Grassland is home to occasional moose, white-tailed deer,
sharp-tailed grouse, prairie chickens and a wide variety of other plants and animals, many
of them rare. The western prairie fringed orchid is listed as a threatened plant. Several
butterflies that find home on this unit are also of concern. A good deal of natural sandy
blow-outs occur on this grassland, which is surrounded by intensive cultivation. This
large contiguous tallgrass prairie unit is particularly significant since tallgrass is so
rare on the Great Plains.

_Landscape Conditions on and near the Sheyenne National Grassland_

� Overall, only four percent of all native tallgrass prairie on the Northern Great
Plains remains extant, mostly in small, scattered fragments, which increases the
likelihood of invasion by undesirable exotic plant species. More than 80 percent of the
area around the Sheyenne National Grassland has been intensively cultivated.

� Fire is less frequent, and flood and grazing patterns have changed over time.

� Approximately 11,000 acres of leafy spurge occur on this grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Sheyenne National Grassland_

� More than 40 rare or sensitive plant species exist on this grassland. The western
prairie fringed orchid is a listed threatened species.

� The prairie chicken is a species of special concern as a result of its decline in
numbers.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on and near the Sheyenne National Grassland_

� The elimination of prairie swales and small-stream floodplains by plowing, draining,
straightening of mid-reaches, and the elimination of riparian vegetation, along with
ditching and levee construction, contribute to the loss of native species and the
introduction of non-native species. Remaining wetlands are important to waterfowl and
other species.

_Recreation on the Sheyenne National Grassland_

� The North Country Trail provides horseback riding and hiking opportunities.

� Resource damage is occurring in several areas due to off-highway use.

� Photography, wildflower viewing, and horseback riding are popular activities.

� Big game hunting is popular.

_Economic Environment on the Sheyenne National Grassland_

� Livestock grazing complements other agricultural activities, including the
cultivation of food and feed grains, like soybeans, sunflowers and potatoes.

**Medicine Bow-Routt National Forest Unit:**

**Thunder Basin National Grassland**

The Thunder Basin National Grassland is located in northeastern Wyoming and occupies
about 572,000 acres of land among a mosaic of state, federal and private lands. These
lands generally lie between Douglas on the south, to Newcastle on the east, to Gillette on
the north, to Wright on the west. This unit is administered by the Douglas Ranger
District, Douglas, Wyoming.

The Thunder Basin National Grassland is a blend of mixed-grass grassland, sagebrush
grassland, cottonwood, greasewood and ponderosa pine/juniper vegetation, within rolling
plains, escarpment, dissected plains, and shale upland landscapes. The grassland is home
to pronghorn, prairie elk and prairie dogs. A great deal of coal is also mined on the
grassland, including the largest coal strip-mine operation in the nation, located near
Gillette.

_Landscape Conditions on the Thunder Basin National Grassland_

� Wyoming has the largest grassland area in the Northern Great Plains (approximately
11,600 square miles of good quality with 90 percent of it in grass and shrubland). The
Thunder Basin National Grassland lies within this area.

� Powder River Basin coal has been mined extensively since the 1970. Six large coal
mines currently operate on the grassland.

� Uranium and bentonite reserves exist on the grassland and have been mined at varying
levels over time as market conditions fluctuate.

� Oil and gas is found on the grassland, with exploration and production most recently
booming in the 1970s.

� More than 150 properties are eligible for the National Register of Historic Places;
however, less than one percent of them have been monitored for site conditions over time.

� More than 3,400 acres of leafy spurge and 200 acres of crested wheat grass are found
on this grassland.

� Sizable fossil resources exist on the grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Thunder Basin National Grassland_

� Prairie dog colonies on the national grassland are managed to provide black-footed
ferret habitat. They may provide sufficient habitat for a black-footed ferret
reintroduction area.

� The Forest Service and the three grazing associations work together to manage and
control noxious weeds, including leafy spurge and Canada thistle, using biological and
chemical methods.

� Sage grouse populations are declining regionally and the decline may be caused by
habitat deterioration. Big sagebrush communities provide valuable habitat for sage grouse,
pronghorn, mule deer, and many other wildlife species.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on and near the Thunder Basin National Grassland_

� More than 800 human-made reservoirs have been built, with many of them managed to
maintain or enhance wetland habitat and fisheries.

� Cottonwood floodplains along the Cheyenne River, Antelope Creek and other riparian
habitats across the national grassland provide key wildlife habitat. The cottonwood
floodplain woodlands are regenerating due to improved livestock grazing management.
However, in some of the intermittent and ephemeral draws, adequate woody regeneration is
not yet occurring.

_Recreation on the Thunder Basin National Grassland_

� Recreational opportunities abound, although few developed sites exist. Scattered
ownership patterns and limited legal public access across private lands hinders some
recreational opportunities on public lands.

� Prairie dog shooting is popular and attracts participants from across the nation.

� Roads constructed to service oil and gas facilities provide improved motorized
access for recreationists.

� Hunting pronghorn antelope and mule deer is popular. Upland bird hunting and other
big game hunting, including elk and white-tailed deer, occur as well.

� Largemouth bass and trout fishing is available at several sites on the grassland.

_Economic Environment on the Thunder Basin National Grassland_

� Revenues from the Thunder Basin National Grassland to the local counties exceed $1.5
million each year, mainly from oil and gas leasing; revenues to the State of Wyoming have
exceeded $53 million per year in recent years, mainly due to coal mining.

� Approximately 10 percent of employment in Campbell County is due to coal mining
activities from the Thunder Basin National Grassland (the highest employment of any
affected county in the planning area).

� Forage on the grassland is utilized by more than 180 ranching operations with
approximately 20,000 sheep and 23,000 cattle. An equal number of livestock are associated
with intermingled private and state land.

**Nebraska National Forest Units:**

**Bessey Ranger District and Samuel R. McKelvie National Forest**

About 90,000 acres of Sandhills country make up the Halsey unit of the Bessey District,
located in central Nebraska in Thomas and Blaine Counties. Named after Dr. Charles E.
Bessey, Bessey convinced the federal government to plant pine and other tree species in
the treeless Sandhills. Beginning in 1902, work began in establishing a nursery and
hand-planting a forest across the shifting dunes and grasslands of central Nebraska. The
Bessey Tree Nursery is located within the unit and is administered separately from the
Bessey Ranger District, Halsey, Nebraska.

Named for former Nebraska governor and cattleman S.R. McKelvie, the 117,000-acre Samuel
R. McKelvie National Forest, administered by the Bessey Ranger District, lies in the
Sandhills of northcentral Nebraska in Cherry County. Elevation rises to about 3,200 feet,
and the topography consists of low rolling hills, ridges and grass-covered dunes. The unit
is administered by the Bessey Ranger District. Located in the Nebraska Sandhills, the unit
historically contained mixed-grass prairie. Significant tree planting after the turn of
the century provided a blend of grasslands and plantation forests of mainly ponderosa
pine. The unit is home to sharp-tailed grouse, raptors and the endangered blow-out
penstemon.

_Landscape Conditions on the Bessey Ranger District and the Samuel R. McKelvie_
_National Forest_

� Nebraska's high-quality grassland landscapes exist in the Sandhills region, with
these two sandhill units an excellent example.

**�** Extensive tree plantations have been planted on about 25,000 acres. Many
trees are at 50 to 90 years old and have a high occurrence of insect infestations and
diseases.

� Active wind-eroded sites, known as blowouts, are being stabilized, which reduces
habitat conditions for blowout penstemon, an endangered plant.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Bessey Ranger District and the Samuel R. McKelvie_
_National Forest_

� Riparian woodlands along major rivers are regenerating in most areas.

� Some shrub thickets show excessive browsing and trailing damage by livestock. These
areas provide important habitat for deer and many species of birds.

� Recent changes in livestock management practices have increased habitat for prairie
grouse and associated wildlife species.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Bessey Ranger District and the Samuel R. McKelvie National_
_Forest_

� The High Plains Aquifer lies beneath the surface of the Sandhills. Some water is
withdrawn to support irrigation and other uses outside the units. Aquifer depletion and
contamination due to agricultural or livestock activities do not appear to pose risks on
the units.

_Recreation on the Bessey Ranger District and the Samuel R. McKelvie National Forest_

� Tree plantations provide a special recreational setting in the Sandhills that many
appreciate.

� Horseback riding has been enhanced through development of camping facilities
designed for horse users.

� Grouse hunting is popular and attracts participants from across the nation.

� Birdwatching and wildlife viewing are popular.

� The largest recreational site on the Nebraska National Forest is the Bessey
Recreational Complex. This facility includes camping, a swimming pool, horseriding
facilities, and hiking trails.

� The only active fire tower on the Nebraska National Forest, Scott Fire Tower on the
Bessey Ranger District, provides a vista for viewing the forest.

� Bessey Nursery, the nation's oldest federal nursery, provides of the nursery area.

_Economic Environment on the Bessey Ranger District and the Samuel R. McKelvie_
_National Forest_

� With very little of the area in cropland, livestock grazing is very important to
local lifestyles and the local economy.

� All affected counties (Blaine, Cherry, and Thomas) have increased in economic
diversity the past 15 years. Recreation and livestock grazing contribute to economic
diversity.

**Buffalo Gap National Grassland**

The Buffalo Gap National Grassland is located in southwestern South Dakota, and
includes more than 595,000 acres of land that borders and is intermingled with private,
state, Indian reservation, and national park lands. The eastern half of this unit extends
from near Kadoka, South Dakota, on the east to the Cheyenne River on the west, north to
U.S. Highway 14 and south to the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation. The Wall Ranger District,
Wall, South Dakota, administers the eastern half. The western half extends from the
Cheyenne River on the east to the Wyoming and Nebraska borders on the west and south,
respectively. The Fall River Ranger District, Hot Springs, South Dakota, administers the
western half.

The Buffalo Gap National Grassland contains mixed-grass vegetation. The landscape
includes rolling prairie and badlands topography. The unit is home to many wildlife
species, such as pronghorn antelope, both mule and white-tailed deer, and prairie dogs.
Currently, black-footed ferrets are being reintroduced into Conata Basin. Sizable beds of
agates and vertebrate and invertebrate fossils can be found on the grassland.

In addition, the National Grassland Visitor Center is located in Wall, South Dakota.
The center is administered by the Wall Ranger District, and focuses on interpretation of
the Great Plains and offers information on the country's national grasslands. The Center
features more than 20 exhibits.

_Landscape Conditions on the Buffalo Gap National Grassland_

� Livestock management practices have created diverse areas with relatively high and
patchy coverage of grasses on the eastern portion of this grassland. Due to low
precipitation, the western half is limited in providing prairie grouse nesting habitat.

� At least 8,200 acres of crested wheat grass exists on the grassland.

� Sizable fossil resources exist on the grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Buffalo Gap National Grassland_

� Historic prairie dog acreage has been reduced by 98 percent. Landownership
adjustments to create larger contiguous blocks of public land in selected areas are
providing additional opportunities for conservation of biological diversity found on
prairie dog colonies.

� Efforts to successfully reintroduce the endangered black-footed ferret into prairie
dog colonies are underway.

� Along with adjoining private and tribal lands, the national grassland provides
habitat for some of the few swift fox populations occurring in South Dakota.

� Many wooded draws and riparian woodlands are regenerating under improved management,
but some woodlands continue to decline or fail to regenerate. In addition to loss of
watershed values and recreational values, this also reduces habitat for deer and many
species of birds.

� In prior decades, sagebrush has been sprayed and eliminated in portions of the
national grassland. The remaining sagebrush is important wildlife habitat and a major
contributor to the diversity of plants and animals on the national grassland.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Buffalo Gap National Grassland_

� Improved rangeland management practices on the national grassland are contributing
to improvements in the Bad River, which has been identified as an impaired South Dakota
watershed.

� Sixty percent of the unit's wetlands are associated with stock ponds and
water-diversion developments.

� Emergent vegetation around many small impoundments and natural wetland basins is
substantially reduced or removed by mid-summer by livestock. Improved livestock management
practices are allowing for limited recovery.

� Reduced flooding along the Cheyenne River below Angostura Reservoir is likely
contributing to the decline in cottonwood forests along the river.

� One or more native fish species in the Cheyenne River may become federally listed as
threatened or endangered.

_Recreation on the Buffalo Gap National Grassland_

� This grassland provides excellent backcountry opportunities, including the Cheyenne
River and Redshirt RARE II areas. One non-motorized area, Indian Creek, is also a good
backcountry area.

� Fishing opportunities are rapidly declining as many small reservoirs age and become
more shallow due to siltation.

� Railroad Buttes provides an excellent off-highway vehicle area, but concerns have
been expressed about resource damage and conflicts with other uses.

� Agate hunting, birdwatching and wildlife viewing are popular.

� Big game and upland bird hunting is popular, as is shooting prairie dogs.

_Economic Environment on the Buffalo Gap National Grassland_

� Livestock grazing is an important lifestyle in the affected area. Recent exploration
for oil and gas may contribute to local economies in the Edgemont area.

� All affected counties in the Buffalo Gap National Grassland area have increased in
economic diversity the past 15 years, one of only two national grassland units in the
Northern Great Plains to have had this occur. Tourism likely contributes to the increase
in economic diversity.

**Fort Pierre National Grassland**

The Fort Pierre National Grassland includes more than 115,000 acres of federal land,
which is intermingled with other federal, state and privately held lands. It lies south of
Pierre, South Dakota, north of Interstate 90, and west of the Lower Brule Indian
Reservation. This unit is administered by the Fort Pierre Ranger District, Pierre, South
Dakota.

The Fort Pierre National Grassland consists of mixed-grass vegetation on a rolling hill
landscape just west of the Missouri River. The grassland is home to many species of
wildlife including prairie chicken, sharp-tailed grouse, antelope, mule and white-tailed
deer, and waterfowl.

_Landscape Conditions on the Fort Pierre National Grassland_

� More than 85 percent of the national grassland is summer-grazed by livestock each
year, resulting in fairly uniform cover levels after the grazing season in many areas.
Areas with relatively high and patchy residual cover are limited.

� Big bluestem and other tall warm season grasses are becoming prominent in recent
years due to changes in grazing intensities, practices, and moisture conditions.

� Recent changes in livestock management practices have increased habitat for prairie
grouse and associated wildlife species.

� Expansion of woodland habitats has occurred due to planting, fire suppression, and
changes in the water flow of streams.

� More than 4,300 acres of crested wheat grass occur on this grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Fort Pierre National Grassland_

� Prairie dog colonies have been significantly reduced.

� Some wooded draws continue to decline. Loss of woody draws reduces watershed and
recreational values, and habitat for deer and many species of birds.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Fort Pierre National Grassland_

� Improved rangeland management practices on the national grassland are contributing
to improvements in the Bad River, which has been identified as an impaired South Dakota
watershed.

� Emergent vegetation around many small impoundments and natural wetland basins is
substantially reduced or removed by mid-summer by livestock. Improved livestock management
practices are allowing for limited recovery.

_Recreation on the Fort Pierre National Grassland_

� Prairie chicken and sharp-tailed grouse hunting is popular and attracts participants
from across the nation.

� Viewing prairie chicken and grouse courtship rituals from blinds is popular.

� Big game hunting is popular, as is birdwatching and wildlife viewing.

� Fishing in small impoundments is popular.

_Economic Environment on the Fort Pierre National Grassland_

� About a third of the economic activity in the three county area is due to
government. Only a small percentage of the income and employment is due to livestock
grazing on Fort Pierre National Grassland.

**Pine Ridge Ranger District**

This unit lies in Dawes and Sioux Counties of northwestern Nebraska. Included are the
Pine Ridge area at about 51,000 acres with the Soldier Creek area at about 10,000 acres.
The Pine Ridge Job Corps Center is also located in this district, although it is
administered separately. Elevations rise to 5,000 feet along ridges of ponderosa pine. The
unit is administered by the Pine Ridge Ranger District, Chadron, Nebraska.

The Pine Ridge District is a popular outdoor destination. Its pine-clad forests and
rugged sandstone terrain, rising from the surrounding plains, provide a scenic backdrop
for a number of recreational activities.

_Landscape Conditions on the Pine Ridge Ranger District_

� Fire suppression has allowed ponderosa pine to increase on many areas on the
district. This has created concerns for future forest health and fire risk.

� Timber harvesting on intermingled private lands is occurring.

� About 300 acres of leafy spurge and 400 acres of Canada thistle occur on this
district.

� Less than 10% of the Pine Ridge ecological area is administered by the U.S. Forest
Service.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Pine Ridge Ranger District_

� Annual efforts continue to successfully control leafy spurge and Canada thistle. New
noxious weed infestations could represent a significant threat to native vegetation and
land productivity.

� Exotic grasses, including Kentucky bluegrass, smooth brome and cheatgrass, continue
to slowly spread and replace native plant communities.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Pine Ridge Ranger District_

� Many riparian woodlands along perennial and intermittent streams are regenerating
and in upward trend because of improved management but some are not regenerating because
of bluegrass invasion, livestock grazing and other recreational uses.

_Recreation on the Pine Ridge Ranger District_

� Mountain bike opportunities have improved with the designation of mountain bike
trails.

� Horseback riding has been enhanced through improved trailhead facilities and
on-going construction of the Pine Ridge Trail.

� Hunters have expressed concerns about the lack of public land identification. Big
game and turkey hunting is popular.

� Travel management is a concern, as related to a limited amount of different
recreation settings.

� The Soldier Creek Wilderness and the Pine Ridge National Recreation Area provide
non-motorized opportunities for recreation.

� Recreation conflicts are increasing, such as off road vehicle damage to resources,
vandalism of government property, and increased trails in riparian areas.

_Economic Environment on the Pine Ridge Ranger District_

� Livestock grazing complements other economic activities in Dawes and Sioux Counties.
Approximately one timber sale per decade is sold on the Pine Ridge Ranger District.

**Oglala National Grassland**

The 94,000 acre Oglala National Grassland lies in Dawes and Sioux Counties of
northwestern Nebraska. Topography consists of rolling hills and badlands country. The
grassland is administered by the Pine Ridge Ranger District, Chadron, Nebraska. The
grassland contains mixed-grass vegetation and is home to prairie dogs, pronghorn, mule
deer, raptors, and a variety of ground-nesting birds and reptiles.

_Landscape Conditions on the Oglala National Grassland_

� About 85% of the national grassland is summer-grazed by livestock each year,
resulting in fairly uniform cover levels after the grazing season in many areas. Areas
with relatively high and patchy residual cover are limited.

� Exotic plants increase their range due to excessive or insufficient grazing, fewer
fires, fewer floods, and some recreational activities.

� About 1800 acres of Canada thistle occur on this grassland.

� Sizable fossil resources exist within the badlands of the grassland.

_Terrestrial Ecosystems on the Oglala National Grassland_

� Prairie dog colonies cover small acreages on the national grassland.

� Swift fox habitat occurs in some areas of the national grassland.

� Sagebrush habitat on the national grassland does not naturally occur in abundance,
but is important for wintering pronghorns and other wildlife species.

_Aquatic Ecosystems on the Oglala National Grassland_

� Wetland vegetation around many small impoundments built for livestock watering is
substantially reduced or removed by mid-summer by livestock. Improved livestock management
practices, such as rotation systems, are allowing for recovery.

_Recreation on the Oglala National Grassland_

� Most of the pronghorn hunting on public lands in Nebraska is provided by this
grassland unit.

� Turkey, mule deer, and whitetail deer hunting is popular.

� The Toadstool Geologic Park provides camping and hiking in a badlands environment.
It also has a prehistoric "trackway" from the Oligocene (about 30 million years
ago).

� The Hudson-Meng Bison Bonebed Site provides interpretation of extinct bison and life
in this part of the Great Plains about 10,000 years ago.

_Economic Environment on the Oglala National Grassland_

� Livestock grazing on this grassland unit contributes to the agricultural lifestyle
of the surrounding area. Oil and gas activities may contribute to future economic
