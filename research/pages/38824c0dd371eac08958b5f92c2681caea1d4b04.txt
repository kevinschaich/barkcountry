# Where Reforestation Begins: A First Look at the Savannah River Site’s Future Forest

_Joe Orosz_,
_Southern Appalachian/ Savannah River Site Forest_

_August 21, 2026_

Image

[![Forest Service employees inspect loblolly pine seedlings at the PRT Nursery in Windsor, South Carolina.](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/nfs/files/styles/medium/public/r08/savannah-river/image/Nursery%20image%208-20-26.png?itok=7yo_Cbdj)](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/nfs/files/r08/savannah-river/image/Nursery%20image%208-20-26.png)

Forest Service employees inspect loblolly pine seedlings at the PRT Nursery in Windsor, S.C.

_(USDA Image)_

On a humid Carolina morning, U.S. Forest Service staff from the Savannah River Site Forest walked the rows of seedling beds at PRT Nursery in nearby Windsor, S.C., inspecting the next generation of trees destined to take root across one of the most actively managed forests in the South. For more than 75 years, the Savannah River Site has been a proving ground for large‑scale forest restoration. Today’s visit was a chance to see the future, thousands of pine seedlings grown from carefully selected seed sources, including longleaf cones collected right here on the Savannah River Site Forest.

Reforestation is not just a routine part of forest management, it's a cornerstone of how the Forest Service restores resilience on a working landscape like the Savannah River Site Forest that must support wildlife, withstand storms, and meet the unique demands of a national security mission. Each regeneration harvest kicks off a precise sequence of treatments: site preparation to knock back competitive hardwoods, prescribed fire to clean up debris and prepare fresh seedbeds, and both hand and machine planting operations to ensure each stand is stocked with the right species at the right density. It’s intensely hands‑on work — but it is that hard work that defines long‑term stewardship.

Those steps stretch far beyond planting. The cones collected by staff each fall are sent to specialized extractories to process seeds and test viability. The seed is then stored and sent to nurseries for sowing each spring, based on future planting needs. Nurseries grow high‑quality seedlings through bareroot or containerized methods, which are delivered to the site throughout the planting season. Seedling survival is checked in year one and year three, with foresters hiking plot lines to confirm that the new generation is alive, thriving, and ready to carry the forest forward.

“These seedlings will be ready for delivery later this winter, and we’ll start planting them as soon as soil moisture and temperatures line up,” said Eileen Eck, Forest Silviculturist. “We will ensure seedlings are planted properly to avoid transplant shock and maximize their potential for healthy establishment and growth. Working with nursery staff just a few miles from the forest, along with several others within the region, allows for open and streamlined communication through every step of the process, which is essential to successfully reforest the Savannah River Site.”

Walking through the nursery, seeing next season’s trees swaying lightly in the breeze, it’s clear that reforestation on the Savannah River Site Forest isn’t just about planting trees. It’s about safeguarding a landscape that protects communities, supports local economies, and anchors one of the most important federal missions in the country. These seedlings are more than future trees; they’re the next chapter in a story the Forest Service has been writing here since 1951.
