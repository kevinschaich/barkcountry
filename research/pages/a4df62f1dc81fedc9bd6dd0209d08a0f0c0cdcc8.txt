# Frequently Asked Questions

| ## Questions About Chiricahua Becoming a National Park<br>#### About the Designation<br>- What is the official name now? What does “preserve” mean for management?<br>  <br>  <br>The official name is Chiricahua National Park, as established by H.R.6380, the Chiricahua National Park Act. The law redesignates the entire unit as a national park, and the existing management framework continues. Visitors can find the latest on the official name at nps.gov/chir.<br>- What is the name of the legislation, and when was it signed?<br>  <br>  <br>Congress redesignated Chiricahua through H.R.6380, the Chiricahua National Park Act, which was signed into law on September 30, 2026. The bill was sponsored by Rep. Ciscomani, Juan \[R-AZ-6\] and Rep. Stanton, Greg \[D-AZ-4\].<br>- What is the practical difference between a national monument and a national park?<br>  <br>  <br>Does this change protection or management?The change is primarily one of name and recognition. All units of the National Park System are protected under the same laws and managed under NPS policies, whether they are called monuments, parks, or something else. Chiricahua’s resources receive the same high level of protection today as they will under the new designation. What changes is that “national park” signals the area’s national significance and may draw greater public awareness.<br>- Why was Chiricahua selected now, 100+ years after it was first protected?<br>  <br>  <br>Redesignation is a decision made by Congress. The park was first protected as a national monument in 1924, and Congress has recognized that its volcanic rock spires, “sky island” biodiversity, and Apache and homesteading history merit national park status. We would refer questions about the legislative timing to the bill’s sponsors.<br>#### Boundaries and Land<br>- Did the boundaries change? Is the park still about 12,000 acres?<br>  <br>  <br>The legislation does not change the park’s boundaries, and it remains approximately 12,000 acres.<br>- Does the legislation authorize expansion or land acquisition? What land, from whom?<br>  <br>  <br>The legislation does not include expansion or acquisition authority.<br>- Will any lands be managed differently under this designation (hunting, grazing, other uses)?<br>  <br>  <br>In general, “preserve” units in the National Park System can allow certain activities, such as sport hunting, that are not permitted in national parks, but the specific uses are set out in each law. The majority of Chiricahua National Park is federally designated wilderness which does not allow for the use of guns, motorized vehicles, and activities that disturb wildlife. Existing rules on hunting, grazing, mining, collection, and pets remain in effect. [Special use permit questions are covered here](https://www.nps.gov/chir/planyourvisit/permitsandreservations.htm).<br>- Are private inholdings or neighboring landowners affected?<br>  <br>  <br>We are not aware of any private inholdings that are affected, and the designation does not affect neighboring private land. Landowners with property within or adjacent to the boundary are not affected. The park will continue to work with neighbors as good partners.<br>#### Visitors and Operations<br>- Will entrance fees, camping fees, or permit requirements change?<br>  <br>  <br>Current fees and permits remain in effect. The redesignation does not by itself change fees. Current information is at nps.gov/chir.<br>- Do you expect visitation to increase? Current numbers and projections?<br>  <br>  <br>Redesignations can raise awareness, and some parks have seen increased interest after being renamed, but we aren’t able to make specific projections. In 2025, the park welcomed 70,406 visitors. See more visitor use statistics here: https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics.htm<br>- Is the park prepared to handle more visitors (parking, trails, campgrounds, Bonita Canyon Scenic Drive)?<br>  <br>  <br>The park is prepared to welcome visitors and will monitor use at key areas, including Massai Point, Echo Canyon, Bonita Canyon Campground, parking areas. Currently, the park experiences the highest traffic in March and during the week between Christmas and New Years. We encourage visitors to plan ahead and check [current conditions](https://www.nps.gov/chir/planyourvisit/conditions.htm).<br>- Will there be additional funding or staffing?<br>  <br>  <br>The legislation does not include dedicated funding. Staffing and budget decisions are made through the regular appropriations process. The park will continue to operate within its budget.<br>- Will visitors see new signs, maps, brochures, or a new website? When, and who pays?<br>  <br>  <br>Yes, we are working to update signs, publications, and web content to reflect the new name. Physical signage will be replaced on a phased basis as funding allows. Some existing signs may remain in place during the transition.<br>- Will existing services stay the same?<br>  <br>  <br>Yes. Hiking, camping, scenic drives, and ranger-led programs will continue.<br>- Any plans for new facilities, trails, or visitor experiences?<br>  <br>  <br>No new construction is planned as a result of the redesignation. Any future projects would go through standard planning, environmental review, and public involvement processes.<br>#### Resource Protection<br>- How will the designation affect protection of wildlife, plants, and the “sky island” ecosystem?<br>  <br>  <br>The park’s resources are already protected under federal law and NPS management policies, and that does not change. The redesignation highlights the importance of the Chiricahua landscape, where desert, grassland, and forest ecosystems meet and support exceptional biodiversity. The park will continue its work in resource monitoring, species protection, and research partnerships.<br>- Does the park face threats such as wildfire, drought, climate change, invasive species, or border-related impacts? How will the designation help?<br>  <br>  <br>Like many parks in the Southwest, Chiricahua manages wildfire risk, drought, invasive species, and other issues. The designation does not directly change these challenges, but greater recognition can support partnerships and public engagement.<br>- Will the designation affect dark skies or the International Dark Sky Park status?<br>  <br>  <br>The park is known for some of the darkest night skies in the region. Chiricahua National Monument is an International Dark Sky Park, designated in 2021. The park will continue to protect natural darkness through lighting management and night-sky programs. The redesignation does not change that commitment.<br>#### Cultural and Tribal Connections<br>- How are Chiricahua Apache descendants and tribal nations connected?<br>  <br>  <br>In addition to being the homeland of the Chiricahua Apache, many tribes have a connection to the park. The park values its relationships with the tribes connected to this area.<br>Ak-Chin Indian Community<br>Apache Tribe of Oklahoma<br>Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation<br>Fort Sill Apache Tribe<br>Gila River Indian Community<br>Hopi Tribe of Arizona<br>Jicarilla Apache Nation<br>Kickapoo Tribe of Oklahoma<br>Mescalero Apache Tribe<br>Pascua Yaqui Tribe<br>Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community<br>San Carlos Apache Tribe<br>Southern Ute Tribe of the Southern Ute Reservation<br>Tohono O’odham Nation<br>Tonto Apache Tribe<br>Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation<br>Ute Mountain Ute Tribe<br>White Mountain Apache<br>Yavapai-Apache Nation<br>Zuni Tribe of the Zuni Reservation<br>- Will tribes have a greater role in management, consultation, or interpretation?<br>  <br>  <br>The park will continue government-to-government consultation as required by law and policy.<br>- What will happen with historic structures like Faraway Ranch, Stafford Cabin, the Visitor Center, the Sugarloaf Fire Tower, and other areas on the National Register of Historic Places?<br>  <br>  <br>Allof the park’s historic structures will continue to be preserved and interpreted inline with the National Register of Historic Places and National Park Service tradition. The redesignation does not change their protected status.<br>#### Local Economy and Community<br>- How will this affect tourism and the economy in Willcox, Cochise County, and the region?<br>  <br>  <br>We expect the designation to raise the area’s profile and may encourage more visitors to explore Willcox and the surrounding region. NPS visitor spending studies show that park visitors contribute to local economies through lodging, food, and retail. In 2025, the park welcomed 70,406 visitors. We cannot speculate on specific economic outcomes.<br>- What have local businesses, residents, and officials said?<br>  <br>  <br>The City of Wilcox is supportive of the designation. We look forward to continuing our partnership with the city and the Willcox Chamber of Commerce, Cochise County tourism offices, Arizona Office of Tourism, Western National Parks (WNP), Arizona Desert National Park Partnership (AZNPP), and nearby sites such as Fort Bowie National Historic Site, Coronado National Memorial and neighboring public lands.<br>- Any concerns about overcrowding, rising housing costs, or impacts on small-town character?<br>  <br>  <br>We understand that communities near parks sometimes have questions about growth. The Park Service plans to continue working with local partners and will monitor visitor use and conditions in the park.<br>- Is there a plan to work with the Willcox community, tourism groups, or nearby attractions?<br>  <br>  <br>The park has long partnered with the Willcox Chamber of Commerce, Cochise County tourism offices, Arizona Office of Tourism, Western National Parks (WNP), Arizona Desert National Park Partnership (AZNPP), and nearby sites such as Fort Bowie National Historic Site, Coronado National Memorial and neighboring public lands.<br>#### Broader Context<br>- How does this fit into NPS’s broader priorities?<br>  <br>  <br>The designation recognizes Chiricahua’s national significance, and aligns with the Park Service’s mission to preserve unimpaired the natural and cultural resources of the National Park System for the enjoyment of this and future generations. The designation also aligns Donald J. Trump’s Executive Order 14314, Making America Beautiful Again by Improving Our National Parks, and the Trump Administration’s goal of expanding opportunities to enjoy America’s national treasures.<br>- How many national parks are there now, and is Chiricahua the newest?<br>  <br>  <br>There are 63 officially designated national parks in the United States, while the broader National Park Service system includes 433 total units such as national monuments, historic sites, and recreation areas. Chiricahua is the newest, making it the 64th national park.<br>- Has NPS dealt with similar redesignations recently?<br>  <br>  <br>Yes. Recent examples include Indiana Dunes (2019), Gateway Arch (2018) in St. Louis, Missouri, New River Gorge (2020) in West Virginia, and White Sands (2019) in New Mexico.<br>- Given NPS budget and staffing challenges, how will the agency support a newly redesignated park?<br>  <br>  <br>The Park Service is committed to caring for every park, including Chiricahua. The redesignation does not by itself change the park’s funding. NPS will continue to prioritize safety, resource protection, and visitor services and to work closely with our partners and volunteers. Congress determines park designations and funding. The redesignation recognizes Chiricahua’s significance, and our job is to care for it and welcome visitors.<br>## Visitor Questions<br>#### How do you say the name?<br>Chiricahua is pronounced cheery - cow - ah. Think of a cheery cow instead of a happy cow.<br>#### What does Chiricahua mean?<br>Chiricahua comes from an Opata word meaning wild turkey according to Jesuit Juan Nentvig (writing in the mid-1700s). This mountain range was originally known for all the [wild turkeys](https://www.nps.gov/chir/learn/nature/goulds-turkey.htm) found here. <br>#### When is the busy season?<br>March is the busiest month! Arizona's winter residents and their visitors keep the park especially busy during school breaks in October, December, and March. Parking can be challenging on weekends and holidays from October through April. Unlike many park sites, summer has the fewest visitors.<br>#### Should I bring my dog?<br>Dogs are prohibited on many of the trails, cannot be left unattended, must be leashed where allowed, and cannot be tied out in the park. As a result, many visitors feel they can see and do more if they leave their pets at home.<br>The trail through the trees and meadow from the campground to the historic district allows dogs on a leash. [The hiking guide shows the few trails where dogs are permitted.](https://www.nps.gov/chir/planyourvisit/upload/2026_Park_Hiking_Guide_8x14_Remediated.pdf)<br>#### Can I use bikes, UTVs, or other recreational gear on the trails?<br>No. Most of the park is designated wilderness. Park trails are designed exclusively for hiking and horseback riding.<br>To ensure visitor safety and protect the landscape, other recreational activities are not permitted due to the following hazards and limitations:<br>- Bicycles, E-Bikes, & UTVs: Prohibited due to steep grades, numerous stairs, narrow trails, and sheer cliff edges.<br>- Rock Climbing: Prohibited because the park's rock formations are highly unstable and hazardous.<br>- Winter Sports (Skiing/Snowboarding): Not supported as our local climate does not provide enough snow.<br>#### Where is the closest gas station or restaurant?<br>The closest gas stations are in Sunizona, 27 miles southwest of the park, or in Willcox, 37 miles northwest. Restaurants, soft drinks, ice, and other grocery items are also in Willcox, on Kansas Settlement, or Sunizona.<br>#### Does it snow in the Chiricahua Mountains?<br>Yes. Storms will drop a few inches at a time in lower elevations and up to a foot at higher elevations. Snow could last a week before it melts. You could expect snow anytime between November and April.<br>#### Can I see waterfalls or rivers?<br>Traditionally the creeks run in spring during snow melt and late summer after heavy monsoon rains. The park doesn't have predictable or constant streams and creeks. |
